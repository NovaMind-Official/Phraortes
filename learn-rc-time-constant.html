The RC time constant, explained

A resistor and a capacitor in series fill up like a bucket with a narrow tap. The time constant tells you how fast.

CIRCUITS · 4 MIN READ

Try it: change V, R and C and watch the curve

Definition

For a resistor R and a capacitor C in series, the time constant is

τ = R × C

With R in ohms and C in farads, τ comes out in seconds. A bigger resistor limits the current more, and a bigger capacitor holds more charge, so both slow things down.

Charging

Connect the series pair to a source V. The capacitor voltage and the current follow

V_C(t) = V · (1 − e−t/τ)
I(t) = (V / R) · e−t/τ

At the instant you connect, the capacitor is empty, so all of V drops across the resistor and the current is its largest, V/R. The current then falls away while the voltage climbs.

How far after how long?

TimeCapacitor charged to
1 τ63.2 %
2 τ86.5 %
3 τ95.0 %
4 τ98.2 %
5 τ99.3 %

That is why engineers say a capacitor is "fully charged" after about five time constants.

Worked example

Take V = 9 V, R = 5 kΩ and C = 200 µF.

τ = 5000 Ω × 0.0002 F = 1 s

The starting current is 9 V ÷ 5000 Ω = 1.8 mA. After one second the capacitor sits at about 63 % of 9 V, which is roughly 5.7 V. The energy it stores at full charge is ½CV² = ½ × 200 µF × (9 V)² = 8.1 mJ.

Discharging

If you then let the capacitor discharge through the resistor, the voltage simply decays:

V_C(t) = V₀ · e−t/τ

After one τ it has dropped to about 37 % of where it started.
